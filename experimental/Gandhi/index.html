Gandhi Goes to Pakistan

How Textbooks Constructed Mahatma Gandhi for the Pakistani Collective

While textbooks ostensibly exist to impart historical knowledge through mass education, they primarily function as legitimation instruments for the state to manufacture citizens by fixing an internal “we” against an external “other”. This is especially true for a state like Pakistan, whose existence rests on an ideological proposition. This proposition required restatement after 1971, when the argument that shared religion makes one nation was falsified by the secession of East Pakistan. The curriculum and textbooks that followed served as a state-led response to assert a singular, oppositional religious identity.

The nation-building purpose of these textbooks leaves no register for doubt or historical nuance, leading to a flattened representation of facts. Instead of a historiography of events driven by complex political bargaining, the curriculum constructs a hagiography of black-and-white narratives, where sanctified heroes exist purely in opposition to the flat villains who foil them.

One such example of this is Mahatma Gandhi, a central figure of the Indian freedom movement, acting as the structural foil to the biography of Muhammad Ali Jinnah. We trace how he is represented across 808 sentences in the PCTB’s publications from 1956 to 2022, to observe exactly how the Pakistani state calibrates its founding narrative.

I

The Early Gandhi

The earliest appearance of Gandhi within the digitized corpus is found in a 1956 textbook: New Intermediate Composition, published for Class 12 by the Punjab Advisory Board for Books (a precursor to the PCTB). Published just nine years after Partition, this text predates the creation of Pakistan Studies as a subject.

In the 1950s, the state syllabus operated within a secular, pluralistic framework inherited from British-Indian university boards. Mass secondary education focused on functional English composition, general history, and civics.

In this textbook, Gandhi appears neutral, pragmatic, and entirely devoid of theological opprobrium. He is utilized purely as an illustrative example for English grammar and idiomatic composition.

The characterization of Gandhi as a “great diplomat” reflected a standard, secular historical consensus across South Asian education before the nationalization and Islamization reforms of the 1970s.

“To go without saying (Something that is taken for granted). It goes without saying that Mr. Gandhi was a great diplomat.”

— New Intermediate Composition, Class 12, 1956, p. 66

Later in the same volume, within a biographical sketch of Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Gandhi appears as a respected peer and colleague in the anti-colonial constitutional struggle.

“[Jinnah] joined hands with Mr. Gandhi and demanded substantial amount of self-government from the British Government in 1919.”

— New Intermediate Composition, Class 12, 1956, p. 306

  1. Following the historic 1916 Lucknow Pact brokered by Jinnah between Congress and the All-India Muslim League, Jinnah and Gandhi maintained close cooperation in challenging colonial autocracy and negotiating provincial devolution under the Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms.
  2. At this juncture, Jinnah was simultaneously a prominent leader in both the Congress and the Muslim League. Both leaders united against the imperial government’s wartime repression, particularly the Rowlatt Act of 1919, which prompted Jinnah’s famous resignation from the Imperial Legislative Council.

Gandhi’s initial willingness to cooperate with the 1919 reforms is also acknowledged in a 1975 Class 7 Social Studies textbook that was published during the transitional window of Bhutto’s educational reforms.

“Although these reforms were inadequate, Mr. Gandhi thought that they should be accepted and given a fair trial. He did not want to start any movement against the British at that time; rather he wanted to co-operate with them and make the new reforms a success. But he was compelled to start a movement against the Government on account of the high-handedness of the Government and the great zeal of the Muslim leaders.”

— Social Studies: History and Civics, Class 7, 1975, p. 89

Prior to 1920, Gandhi operated as an imperial constitutionalist who had recruited soldiers for the British Army during WWI (earning the Kaisar-i-Hind Medal) and advocated giving the Montagu–Chelmsford reforms a fair trial at the Amritsar Congress in December 1919.

His decisive turn toward mass non-cooperation occurred during 1919–1920 in direct response to imperial violence that culminated in the Rowlatt Act (March 1919) authorizing detention without trial and the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre in Amritsar on 13 April 1919.

II

Ally of the Khilafat Movement: Cynicism, Opportunism, and Ruin

Following the secession of East Pakistan in December 1971, the narrative of the Non-Cooperation and Khilafat alliance underwent a radical transformation. The emergence of sovereign Bangladesh challenged the foundational state premise that shared religious adherence automatically guarantees political cohesion.

Seeking to prove retrospectively that inter-communal political partnership between Hindus and Muslims was intrinsically flawed from the outset, the post-1971 curriculum reframed the Khilafat Movement as a sacred, pan-Islamic anti-imperial struggle by South Asian Muslims to preserve the Ottoman Caliphate and protect the holy sanctuaries of Mecca and Medina following the Treaty of Sèvres. Gandhi’s entry into this movement was reframed as an exercise in cold, calculating exploitation instead of an act of anti-colonial solidarity.

In the flagship post-1971 textbooks, Gandhi’s entry into the Khilafat agitation is depicted not as an act of anti-colonial solidarity, but as an exercise in cold, calculating exploitation.

“The British Government had imprisoned all the prominent Muslim leaders of the movement... Under these circumstances, Mr. Gandhi availed of this opportunity and started supporting the movement. He had his own axe to grind in joining this movement.”

— Social Studies VI, Class 6, 1994, p. 150

Gandhi advocated supporting the Khilafat cause as early as the Delhi Khilafat Conference in November 1919, arguing that Hindu solidarity with a legitimate Muslim grievance was an unconditional moral imperative. He termed it “an opportunity of uniting Hindus and Muslims as would not arise in a hundred years.” When orthodox Hindu leaders urged Gandhi to condition Khilafat support on Muslim concessions regarding cow slaughter, Gandhi adamantly refused, insisting that true friendship between communities must never be transactional.

The Non-Cooperation program was jointly adopted by the Central Khilafat Committee and the Indian National Congress as a mutual weapon against British imperialism following the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the Treaty of Sèvres.

In Urdu editions across the 1970s and 1980s, the phrasing is identical. In Urdu historiography, describing Gandhi’s support as “غنیمت جان کر” (ghanīmat jān kar, “taking advantage of a windfall”) suggests a notion of cynical exploitation.

مسٹر گاندھی نے اِس موقع کو غنیمت جان کر تحریک خلافت کی ابتدا میں حمایت تو شروع کردی مگر بعد میں اچانک کنارہ کشی اختیار کر لی جس سے تحریک کے مقاصد کو خاصا نقصان پہنچا۔

(“Mr. Gandhi, seizing the occasion as a boon, began by supporting the Khilafat Movement, but later suddenly withdrew, which did considerable damage to the movement's aims.”)

— Social Studies, Class 6, 1978, p. 139

In reality, Gandhi staked his entire national leadership on the Khilafat cause, fiercely debating skeptical Congress colleagues (such as C. R. Das and Lala Lajpat Rai) at the Special Calcutta Congress (September 1920) to ensure official Congress adoption of Khilafat demands.

The eventual suspension of Non-Cooperation in February 1922 applied to all Indian anticolonial agitations nationwide, not just Muslim branches, and was triggered by bloodshed at Chauri Chaura.

The 2015 Class 8 English edition takes this transactional charge further, asserting that Gandhi usurped leadership purely for personal fame and Hindu benefit while Muslim leaders were imprisoned.

“Gandhi assumed the leadership of the movement, as all Muslim leaders were behind the bars. He used all the energies of Muslims for his personal repute and the benefit of Hindus.”

— History 8, Class 8, 2015, p. 36

  1. Gail Minault, B. R. Nanda, and M. Naeem Qureshi establish that Gandhi’s commitment to the Khilafat cause was rooted in a genuine anticolonial philosophy of mutual solidarity. Gandhi publicly insisted that Hindu support for a legitimate Muslim religious grievance was an unconditional moral duty.
  2. Gandhi was elected President of the All-India Khilafat Conference in Delhi (23–24 November 1919) while the Ali brothers (Mohamed Ali Jauhar and Shaukat Ali) were interned. However, following their release under royal amnesty in December 1919, Mohamed Ali and Shaukat Ali took executive command of the movement (Mohamed Ali serving as President of the Central Khilafat Committee).
  3. Throughout 1920–1922, Gandhi campaigned across India side-by-side with Mohamed Ali, Shaukat Ali, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, and Maulana Abdul Bari of Firangi Mahal as equal partners in a unified national front against British imperialism.

The Hijrat Movement Accusation: Economic Ruin and Conspiracy

In the later editions the critique of Gandhi escalates from opportunism into an accusation of deliberate economic sabotage.

In the 2015 Class 8 History and the 2019 Class 12 Pakistan Studies texts, Gandhi is charged with orchestrating the disastrous Hijrat Movement that ruined thousands of Indian Muslim families.

“On the instigation of Gandhi some Muslim Ulema declared India as Dar-ul-Harb and advised the Muslims to migrate to Afghanistan.”

— History 8, Class 8, 2015, p. 36

In the 2019 Class 12 text, this becomes a full subsection titled “Gandhi’s Role” (Gāndhī kā Kirdār), claiming Gandhi induced Muslims to sell their properties for a pittance and migrate to Afghanistan, revealing his “true face”.

گاندھی کا کردار: گاندھی نے تحریک خلافت کے جلسوں میں کھل کر مسلمانوں کی حمایت کی اور مکمل تعاون کا یقین دلایا۔ اس کے بعد گاندھی کے مشورے پر مسلمانوں نے خطابات اور سرکاری ملازمتوں سے استعفے دیئے، ٹیکس دینے سے انکار کیا، کونسلوں کا بائیکاٹ کیا اور اپنے گھر بار، جائیدادیں اور مویشی اونے پونے بیچ کر افغانستان، ایران یا کسی دوسرے اسلامی ملک ہجرت کرنے لگے۔ لاکھوں مسلمان اپنے گھر بار چھوڑ کر افغانستان کی سرحد پر پہنچے لیکن افغان حکومت نے انہیں داخلے کی اجازت نہ دی۔ وہ بے یار و مددگار بھوک پیاس سے تڑپ تڑپ کر مرنے لگے۔ تب جا کر مسلمانوں کو گاندھی کا اصل چہرہ نظر آیا۔

(“Gandhi’s Role: Gandhi openly supported Muslims in Khilafat rallies and assured full cooperation. Thereafter, on Gandhi’s advice, Muslims resigned titles and government jobs, refused taxes, boycotted councils, and sold properties and livestock for a pittance to migrate to Afghanistan... Helpless, they died in agony of hunger and thirst. Only then did Muslims see Gandhi’s true face.”)

— Mutāla‘ah Pākistān 12, Class 12, 2019, p. 16

  1. The Hijrat Movement unfolded between May and August 1920 (Summer 1920), sparked by the publication of the harsh peace terms of the Treaty of Sèvres (May 1920) and Afghan Amir Amanullah Khan’s initial public speech offering asylum to Indian Muslim refugees. It collapsed in mid-August 1920 when the Afghan government, overwhelmed by the influx, closed its borders at the Khyber Pass.
  2. The theological declaration that British India had become Dār al-Ḥarb (an Abode of War) requiring emigration (hijrat) was formulated exclusively by Muslim religious scholars including Maulana Abdul Bari of Firangi Mahal and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad. Gandhi had neither the religious authority nor the institutional standing to issue Islamic fatwas.
  3. The practical advice on routes and logistics was issued by the Central Khilafat Committee’s Hijrat branch offices in Delhi, Rawalpindi, and Peshawar. While Gandhi defended the emigrants’ moral right to non-violent protest in the journal Young India, he did not invent or engineer the migration.
  4. Historical records (railway passes and British colonial intelligence) confirm total emigrants numbered between 30,000 and 60,000 (~18,000 registered at Jamrud), contradicting the textbook claim of “hundreds of thousands” (lākhon).
III

The Chauri Chaura Incidence: Betrayal at the Cusp of Victory

The termination of the Non-Cooperation and Khilafat alliance is narrated across textbooks as an act of unilateral betrayal. In February 1922, violence erupted in Gorakhpur district, United Provinces, leading to the abrupt end of the joint agitation.

The 1986 History textbook claims Gandhi used the burning of the Chauri Chaura police station as a mere “pretext” to call off the Civil Disobedience Movement.

چوری چورا کے مقام پر ایک مشتعل ہجوم نے تھانے کو آگ لگا دی جس میں اکیس سپاہی جل کر مر گئے۔ گاندھی نے اس واقعے کو بہانہ بنا کر سول نافرمانی کی تحریک ختم کرنے کا اعلان کر دیا۔

(“At a place called Chauri Chaura, an enraged mob set fire to a police station in which twenty-one policemen were burned to death. Gandhi used this incident as a pretext to announce the end of the Civil Disobedience Movement.”)

— Tārīkh-e-Pākistān, Class 9–10, 1986, p. 244

The movement suspended in February 1922 was the Non-Cooperation Movement (Adam-e-Ta‘awun). The Civil Disobedience Movement (Civil Na-farmani) did not begin until March 1930 with the Salt Satyagraha, eight years later.

Gandhi viewed non-violence (ahimsa) not as a political maneuver, but as the foundational spiritual prerequisite of satyāgraha (“soul-force” / “holding firmly to truth”). When the mob killed 22 policemen at Chauri Chaura on 4 February 1922, Gandhi undertook an immediate five-day penitential fast (12–16 February 1922) and halted the mass campaign because he believed the country was descending into uncontrolled mob violence that would justify ruthless colonial retaliation.

This withdrawal is framed as a devastating blow to the Muslims: textbooks claim Gandhi and his Hindu associates walked away, severely damaging the Khilafat cause.

چوری چورا کے واقعے کے بعد گاندھی اور اس کے ہندو ساتھی تحریک خلافت سے الگ ہو گئے جس سے تحریک کو شدید نقصان پہنچا۔

(“After the Chauri Chaura incident, Gandhi and his Hindu associates separated from the Khilafat Movement, which severely damaged the movement.”)

— Tārīkh-e-Pākistān, Class 9–10, 1986, p. 246

  1. Gandhi did not politically “separate” from Khilafat. Less than a month after the Bardoli resolution (passed by the Congress Working Committee in Bardoli, Gujarat, on 11–12 February 1922), on 10 March 1922, the British arrested Gandhi for sedition, sentenced him to six years in prison, and incarcerated him in Yerwada Central Jail until February 1924.
  2. The Khilafat Movement collapsed not because Hindus walked away, but because on 3 March 1924, President Mustafa Kemal Atatürk and the Turkish Grand National Assembly officially abolished the Ottoman Caliphate, exiled the Caliph, and secularized the Republic of Turkey. The textbooks systematically omit this Turkish decree.

The 1975 Pakistan Studies textbook asserts that anti-colonial victory was imminent and that the British were on the verge of surrender when Gandhi decided to discontinue the campaign.

“The British rulers were on the verge of surrender when all of a sudden Gandhi decided to discontinue the non-cooperation movement on the pretext of violence at Chauri Chaura.”

— Pakistan Studies 9–10, Class 9–10, 1975, p. 162

The British colonial state in 1922 commanded overwhelming military, police, and institutional power. Viceroy Lord Reading had already prepared sweeping emergency crackdowns; colonial authorities were waiting for outbreaks of civil violence to crush the nationalist movement with unsparing martial force.

In his published essay “The Crime of Chauri Chaura” (Young India, 16 February 1922), Gandhi recognized that if the movement turned violent, the British would unleash modern mechanized force against an unarmed populace, just as they had done at Jallianwala Bagh in 1919.

IV

The Interwar Ashram Years, Kohat Riots, and the Communal Pull

Following his premature release from Yerwada Central Jail in February 1924 after an emergency appendectomy, Gandhi returned to public life to find the fragile inter-communal unity of the Khilafat and Non-Cooperation campaigns in ruins. Gandhi largely withdrew from active electoral and agitational politics, retreating to his Satyagraha Ashram at Sabarmati on the outskirts of Ahmedabad.

He turned his energies toward what he termed the “Constructive Programme”—village sanitation, the promotion of hand-spun cloth (khadi), basic craft education, and the moral crusade against untouchability. The textbooks introduce Gandhi’s interwar ashram years immediately following a description of the militant Hindu Mahasabha (Pakistan Studies 9–10, 1975, p. 167), grouping the Indian National Congress, Gandhi, and the Hindu Mahasabha under a joint omnibus section heading “The Congress and Hindu Mahasabha”.

The textbook does not document or assert that Gandhi joined, was funded by, or took ideological instructions from the Hindu Mahasabha.

Instead it relies on editorial juxtaposition and guilt-by-association: grouping the militant Mahasabha’s anti-Muslim expulsion campaigns and Gandhi’s ashram retreat under a single joint title, alleging he “used to keep quiet” during communal riots.

“Gandhi was arrested at the conclusion of the non-cooperation movement and sentenced for sedition... Whenever Gandhi's attention was drawn towards Hindu-Muslim riots he used to keep quiet.”

— Pakistan Studies 9–10, Class 9–10, 1975, p. 167

  1. In reality, Gandhi was an ideological opponent of the Hindu Mahasabha and RSS. Mahasabha ideologues including V. D. Savarkar, Dr. B. S. Moonje, and Bhai Parmanand despised Gandhi’s doctrine of ahimsa (non-violence) as an “emasculating perversion” of Hindu martial strength and vehemently condemned his defense of Muslim minority rights as “treasonous appeasement.”
  2. This enmity culminated lethally on 30 January 1948, when Nathuram Godse, a militant affiliated with the Hindu Mahasabha and RSS, assassinated Gandhi in New Delhi as a direct consequence of Gandhi undertaking a fast unto death protecting Delhi Muslims and forcing the Indian government to release Rs 55 crore to Pakistan.
  3. When catastrophic communal riots erupted in Kohat (NWFP) in September 1924, resulting in dozens of casualties, far from indifferent, Gandhi was devastated. On 18 September 1924, he commenced a 21-day fast for Hindu–Muslim unity, staged at the private residence of his close Muslim comrade, Maulana Mohammad Ali Jauhar in Delhi, attended by Hakim Ajmal Khan, Dr. Mukhtar Ahmed Ansari, and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad.
  4. As a direct consequence of Gandhi’s intervention, the National Unity Conference (26 September 1924) was convened, where over 300 interfaith delegates passed binding resolutions defending minority religious freedom and establishing arbitration boards.

On the very next page of the same textbook (p. 168), we are told that Gandhi subordinated Indian independence to anti-Muslim dietary taboos, having the least regard for Muslim interests.

“A great change was taking place in the mind of Gandhi also. He had the least regard for the interest of the Muslims. He used to say openly that the problem of cow protection was as important as that of independence.”

— Pakistan Studies 9–10, Class 9–10, 1975, p. 168

It is historically true that Gandhi revered the cow as a profound spiritual symbol. In Young India (6 October 1921 & 29 May 1924) and his presidential address to the Belgaum Cow Conference (December 1924), Gandhi wrote that cow protection (go-seva) was “the dearest possession of the Hindu heart” and “one of the most wonderful phenomena in human evolution,” symbolizing humanity’s cosmic kinship with the voiceless animal kingdom.

The claim that Gandhi used this to harm or disregard Muslim interests is, however, false. Gandhi insisted repeatedly and uncompromisingly that Hindus had absolutely no right to stop Muslims from slaughtering cows by law or force: “I have said that the Hindu must not compel the Mussalman to abstain from cow-slaughter. He must not use force. If he does, he commits a breach of the fundamental tenets of Hinduism... How can I force my will upon others in the name of religion? In India no law can be made to ban cow-slaughter” (Young India, 29 May 1924; Harijan, 25 July 1947).

Gandhi explicitly stated that saving a cow by fighting with or alienating a Muslim neighbor was a sin against Hinduism itself: “To quarrel with the Mussalman, that is, to kill a human being for the sake of saving a cow, is the reverse of Hinduism” (Young India, 12 October 1921).

V

Round Table Conferences

As the 1920s drew to a close, the fragile constitutional status quo started fracturing under the weight of irreconcilable political demands. The appointment of the all-British Indian Statutory Commission (the Simon Commission) in November 1927 without Indian representation sparked nationwide boycotts. In response, an All-Parties Conference drafted the Nehru Report (1928) under Motilal Nehru, which advocated unitary dominion status, adult suffrage, and joint electorates, while summarily rejecting separate electorates, weightage, and residual provincial autonomy.

At the All-Parties Convention in Calcutta in December 1928, Jinnah proposed his amendments to safeguard Muslim provincial representation. When the Congress leadership rejected these amendments, Jinnah delivered his famous Fourteen Points of March 1929, marking his separation from the Congress. With constitutional negotiations collapsed, the Congress convened at Lahore in December 1929 under Jawaharlal Nehru’s presidency, who unfurled the tricolor flag on the banks of the Ravi and declared Purna Swaraj (Complete Independence).

In March 1930, Gandhi ended his six-year ashram seclusion to launch the Civil Disobedience Movement, inaugurating it with the 241-mile Salt March from Sabarmati to Dandi. On 5 May 1930, British authorities arrested Gandhi, incarcerating him without trial in Yerwada Central Jail. It was in the midst of nationwide civil unrest, mass arrests, and imperial crisis that the British government convened the London Round Table Conferences to negotiate the constitutional framework for British India.

In its depiction of the London Round Table Conferences, the textbooks construct Gandhi as an obstinate obstructionist who stayed away out of political cunning.

“Mr. Gandhi was a shrewd politician. He knew that by staying away from the Conference, he could dictate his terms, which he did.”

— Social Studies 8, Class 8, 1980, p. 75

Gandhi did not stay away to “dictate terms” or out of political cunning. Instead, on 5 May 1930, British authorities had arrested Gandhi during the Salt Satyagraha. The First Round Table Conference opened in London on 12 November 1930 and concluded on 19 January 1931, entirely while Gandhi was in jail.

He was only released on 26 January 1931 by Viceroy Lord Irwin to begin negotiations that culminated in the Gandhi–Irwin Pact (5 March 1931). Gandhi did travel to London to attend the Second Round Table Conference (September–December 1931) as the sole representative of the Indian National Congress.

The 2015 Class 8 History textbook introduces the claim that Master Tara Singh mandated Gandhi to represent the Sikh community in London and demand the division of Punjab.

“Master Tara Singh mandated Gandhi to represent Sikhs and gave him a charter of 17 demands which included division of Punjab on religious bases.”

— History 8, Class 8, 2015, p. 46

  1. Master Tara Singh, the prominent leader of the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee and Shiromani Akali Dal, did not attend the London Round Table Conferences and never gave Gandhi a mandate to represent the Sikh community.
  2. The Sikhs were independently represented at the Round Table Conferences by their own delegates, Sardar Ujjal Singh and Sardar Sampuran Singh (at the First and Second RTCs), who defended Sikh minority claims and clashed with Congress. A different figure, Sardar Tara Singh (a Patiala High Court judge), attended the Third RTC in 1932.
  3. The proposal to partition Punjab on religious lines (the Azad Punjab scheme) was conceived in 1943 by the Shiromani Akali Dal under Master Tara Singh, formulated specifically as a reaction to the Muslim League’s 1940 Lahore Resolution. In 1931, the Akalis vehemently opposed any division of Punjab. Projecting the 1943 scheme back twelve years to the 1931 RTC is an anachronistic historical invention.

We are told that at the Second Round Table Conference (1931), Gandhi opposed all minority safeguards: creating obstacles at every step and strongly opposing separate electorates.

گاندھی نے ہر قدم پر کانفرنس کے کام میں رکاوٹیں کھڑی کیں۔ اقلیتوں نے جدا گانہ انتخاب کا مطالبہ کیا جس کی گاندھی نے بڑی سخت مخالفت کی۔

(“Gandhi created obstacles in the conference's work at every step. The minorities demanded separate electorates, which Gandhi opposed very strongly.”)

— Tārīkh-e-Pākistān, Class 9–10, 1986, p. 251

  1. Gandhi did not oppose separate electorates for Muslims or Sikhs (which had already been conceded in the 1916 Lucknow Pact and 1919 Act). His opposition was directed specifically against granting separate electorates to the Depressed Classes (Dalits), arguing that treating Untouchables as a separate statutory community would permanently sever them from Hindu society and freeze the caste curse forever.
  2. Dr. B. R. Ambedkar (representing the Depressed Classes), the Aga Khan (representing Muslims), Rao Bahadur A. T. Pannirselvam (Indian Christians), Sir Henry Gidney (Anglo-Indians), and the European commercial community formed a joint coalition (“Minorities Pact”) demanding statutory separate electorates with weightage.
  3. Gandhi offered Muslims full acceptance of their 14-point demands in exchange for supporting joint electorates for other groups, which the Muslim delegation rejected because they were unwilling to abandon their alliance with the Depressed Classes and other minorities. British Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald used the conference impasse to justify imposing the communal settlement by British fiat in the Communal Award of August 1932.

In the history book from 1986 we learn about the clash between Gandhi and Dr. B. R. Ambedkar over the representation of the “Depressed Classes” (Dalits/Untouchables), and Gandhi’s threat of a fast unto death.

اچھوتوں کو الگ اقلیت قرار دینے کی سفارش کی گئی لیکن گاندھی کسی صورت میں اسے ماننے کے لیے تیار نہ تھا... گاندھی نے اعلان کیا کہ اگر برطانوی حکومت نے اچھوتوں کو ہندوؤں سے الگ تسلیم کرنے کا فیصلہ واپس نہ لیا تو وہ مرن برت (بھوک ہڑتال) رکھ لے گا۔

(“It was recommended that Untouchables be declared a separate minority, but Gandhi was not prepared to accept this... Gandhi announced that if the British government did not withdraw its decision, he would undertake a fast unto death.”)

— Tārīkh-e-Pākistān, Class 9–10, 1986, pp. 252–253

On 20 September 1932, while imprisoned in Yerwada Jail, Gandhi began an indefinite fast to protest Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald’s Communal Award, which granted separate electorates to the Depressed Classes (Dalits). Dr. B. R. Ambedkar argued that without separate electorates, Dalit candidates would remain beholden to upper-caste Hindu majorities.

In his writings, Dr. Ambedkar recounted that Gandhi’s fast placed immense moral coercion upon him, as he was threatened with nationwide violent retribution against Dalits across Indian villages if Gandhi died in prison.

The books conclude that Ambedkar was coerced into signing the Poona Pact, by virtue of which Hindus and Untouchables were recognized as “one nation”.

ڈاکٹر امبیدکر پونا کے مقام پر گاندھی سے ملا اور اسے اپنے فیصلے سے آگاہ کیا۔ اس موقعے پر ڈاکٹر امبیدکر اور گاندھی کے درمیان ایک سمجھوتہ ہو گیا جو تاریخ میں 'میثاق پونا' کے نام سے مشہور ہے، جس کی رو سے ہندوؤں اور اچھوتوں کو ایک ہی قوم تسلیم کیا گیا۔

(“Dr. Ambedkar met Gandhi at Poona and informed him of his decision. On this occasion, a compromise was reached... known in history as the 'Poona Pact,' by virtue of which Hindus and Untouchables were recognized as one nation.”)

— Tārīkh-e-Pākistān, Class 9–10, 1986, p. 253

The Poona Pact (24 September 1932) did not declare that Hindus and Untouchables were “one nation.” Instead, it settled on joint electorates with reserved seats.

The agreement granted the Depressed Classes 148 reserved seats in provincial legislatures (more than double the 71 seats allotted under MacDonald’s Communal Award) and 18% of Central Assembly seats, settled through a primary election system.

VI

The Congress Ministries Crisis

1933–1934 brought Gandhi into irreconcilable friction with both conservative caste Hindus and the professional party leadership of the Indian National Congress. Observing the party apparatus descending into electoral factionalism, careerism, and administrative bickering, Gandhi made the decision to formally and irrevocably resign from the primary membership of the Congress party in October 1934, retiring from institutional party politics.

Consequently, when the British Parliament enacted the landmark Government of India Act 1935—introducing provincial autonomy and abolishing dyarchy—and the provincial elections of February 1937 swept the Congress to power in seven (and eventually eight) provinces, Gandhi held no cabinet portfolio, occupied no legislative seat, and was not even an enrolled party member.

The textbooks connect Gandhi directly to anti-Muslim cultural policies under the Congress Provincial Ministries (1937–1939), alleging he harbored an antipathy to the Urdu language.

“Gandhi had a strong antipathy to it [Urdu]. He clearly said that Urdu was a language of the Muslims.”

— Pakistan Studies 9–10, Class 9–10, 1975, p. 180

  1. The claim of “antipathy to Urdu” is a complete historical inversion. Gandhi was the most vocal champion of Hindustani and insisted that India’s national lingua franca must be a composite blend of Hindi and Urdu, written interchangeably in both the Persian (Nasta‘liq) and Devanagari scripts.
  2. In 1942, Gandhi founded the All-India Hindustani Prachar Sabha specifically to promote equal status for Urdu. When the conservative Hindi Sahitya Sammelan (led by Purushottam Das Tandon) demanded Sanskritized Hindi and sought to purge Urdu, Gandhi drafted a famous resignation letter on 25 July 1945, severing all ties with the Hindi establishment and declaring that he would never support a language policy that marginalized Muslims.

Later editions describe the Congress ministries’ educational policy—the Vidya Mandir scheme—as an aggressive assault on Muslim identity, alleging students were forced to hoist Congress flags, sing Bande Mataram, and perform puja before Gandhi’s picture.

تعلیمی اداروں میں روزانہ تعلیم کا آغاز کانگرسی پرچم لہرانے، بندے ماترم گانے اور گاندھی جی کی مورتی کے سامنے پوجا کرنے سے ہوتا تھا۔

(“The daily start of studies in educational institutions began with hoisting the Congress flag, singing Bande Mataram, and performing puja before a picture of Gandhi.”)

— Mu'āshartī 'Ulūm, Class 8, 1998, p. 109

The 1995 textbook ties this directly to Gandhi’s birthday: claiming Congress directed all schools on 2 October 1938 to perform puja before his picture, wear Gandhi caps, and sing Bande Mataram.

2 اکتوبر 1938ء کو ایم کے گاندھی کی سالگرہ کے موقع پر کانگرس نے تمام اسکولوں میں ہدایت کی کہ ان کی تصویر کے سامنے پوجا کی جائے، گاندھی ٹوپی پہنی جائے اور بندے ماترم گایا جائے۔

(“On the occasion of M. K. Gandhi's birthday on 2 October 1938, the Congress directed that all schools perform puja before his picture, wear Gandhi caps, and sing Bande Mataram.”)

— Tārīkh-e-Pākistān, Class 9–10, 1995, p. 265

  1. This textbook passage is taken verbatim from the All-India Muslim League’s Pirpur Report (1938), which compiled Muslim grievances against Congress provincial governance.
  2. Gandhi consistently and publicly condemned any form of idol worship or personal reverence directed toward himself, writing: “I have no desire to be worshipped... It is repulsive to me.”
  3. Gandhi’s educational program—the Wardha Scheme of Basic Education (Nai Talim)—was designed and chaired by Dr. Zakir Husain, focusing on craft-based vocational learning, village hygiene, and communal harmony. The controversial Vidya Mandir scheme was a localized initiative confined to the Central Provinces under Premier Ravi Shankar Shukla.
  4. In October 1937, the Congress Working Committee, guided by Rabindranath Tagore and Gandhi, officially resolved that only the first two non-religious stanzas of Bande Mataram should be sung in national gatherings, specifically dropping the subsequent stanzas invoking the goddess Durga to respect Muslim sensibilities.
VII

The Lahore Resolution & Rejecting the Two-Nation Theory

The experience of Congress rule destroyed the Muslim League’s faith in constitutional minority safeguards within an undivided Indian federation. Three months after the resignation of the Congress ministries, in March 1940 at Minto Park in Lahore, the All-India Muslim League passed the Lahore Resolution demanding independent, sovereign states for South Asian Muslims. In response, Gandhi stepped forward as the preeminent anticolonial opponent of Partition.

According to the textbooks, following the Muslim League’s passage of the Lahore Resolution on 23 March 1940, Gandhi becomes the direct antagonist of the Pakistan demand, opposing the resolution absolutely.

“The Hindu leaders began to express their views against the Resolution. The Resolution was ridiculed. Gandhi and the Hindus opposed the Resolution absolutely.”

— Pakistan Studies 9 (PESRP), Class 9, 2012, p. 26

Gandhi indeed opposed the Lahore Resolution uncompromisingly. In Harijan (6 April 1940), he wrote that Partition was an “untruth,” arguing that South Asian Muslims and Hindus shared the same ethnic, racial, and linguistic roots: “The vast majority of Muslims of India are converts to Islam or are the descendants of converts. They did not become a separate nation as soon as they changed their religion.”

Gandhi’s opposition was rooted in an insistence on composite anti-colonial nationalism, not anti-Muslim animus. In that same April 1940 editorial, Gandhi stated: “If eight crores of Muslims desire it, no power on earth can prevent them from having it... But I must not be a party to it.” The textbooks reduce Gandhi’s philosophical commitment to pluralist unity into communal arrogance (“Gandhi and the Hindus ridiculed it”), erasing the moral debate between composite nationalism and national self-determination.

We are told by the 1975 Pakistan Studies text that Gandhi described the creation of Pakistan as a “sin” and compared partition to cutting a cow into two pieces.

“In 1946, discussing the division of India with the British, Gandhi described the creation of Pakistan as a ‘sin’ and said that dividing the country into India and Pakistan was like cutting a cow into two parts.”

— Pakistan Studies 9–10, Class 9–10, 1975, p. 185

  1. Gandhi did explicitly call Partition a “sin” and frequently employed the surgical metaphor of “vivisection” (e.g., in Harijan, 6 April 1940: “Partition means a patent untruth... Call me in two before you cut India in two”).
  2. However, there is no historical source that attributes Gandhi likening Partition to “cutting a cow into two parts.”
  3. Tellingly, the 2024 Single National Curriculum volume of Urdu Quaid-e-Insha 11–12 (p. 152) reassigns the identical “cutting a cow” quote to Chakravarti Rajagopalachari.
VIII

Quit India, the C.R. Formula, and the Jinnah–Gandhi Talks

As the Second World War expanded dramatically toward India’s borders with the Japanese capture of Singapore, Malaya, and Rangoon in early 1942, the British War Cabinet dispatched Sir Stafford Cripps to Delhi with an offer of postwar dominion status and a provincial opt-out clause. The collapse of the Cripps Mission in April 1942 precipitated two diverging strategies: while Gandhi spearheaded the desperate, all-out rebellion of the Quit India movement, Jinnah and the League consolidated their organizational apparatus and engaged Gandhi in direct constitutional talks.

The textbooks link the failure of the 1942 Cripps Mission directly to Gandhi and Congress, asserting that after rejecting the proposals, Gandhi raised the slogan “Quit India”.

“Gandhi and his political party All Indian National Congress also rejected the proposals... after the departure of Cripps, Gandhi raised the slogan ‘Quit India’.”

— History 8, Class 8, 2015, p. 60

  1. Textbooks omit that the All-India Muslim League Working Committee formally rejected the Cripps proposals on 11 April 1942—the exact same day Congress rejected them. Jinnah and the League rejected Cripps because the draft declaration offered only an ambiguous provincial opt-out clause rather than an explicit, upfront recognition of the sovereign state of Pakistan.
  2. When the All-India Congress Committee passed the Quit India resolution in Bombay on 8 August 1942, Gandhi delivered his famous “Do or Die” speech calling strictly for non-violent resistance. Within hours, in the early morning of 9 August 1942, the colonial government arrested Gandhi and the entire Congress Working Committee. Gandhi remained incarcerated in the Aga Khan Palace until May 1944. The subsequent nationwide unrest was a leaderless reaction to imperial suppression, not Gandhi’s operational directives.

Prison Correspondence and the C.R. Formula (1944)

While detained in the Aga Khan Palace in Poona, Gandhi endorsed the C.R. Formula formulated by C. Rajagopalachari: acknowledging that correspondence between Quaid-e-Azam and Gandhi continued while he was in prison.

“Raj Gopal Acharia and Gandhi finalized the Formula in March 1944... correspondence between Quaid-e-Azam and Gandhi, who was in jail, continued.”

— Pakistan Studies 9 (PESRP), Class 9, 2012, pp. 27–28

In July 1944, Gandhi initiated correspondence directly with Jinnah while imprisoned, seeking a personal meeting to break the constitutional deadlock.

This quotation is a faithful translation of Gandhi’s Gujarati letter sent to Jinnah on 17 July 1944, shortly after Gandhi was released from detention in the Aga Khan Palace on medical grounds.

“My heart was asking me to write you a letter. I can meet you when you wish. Don’t think me the enemy of Islam or the Muslims. I am not only the friend and servant of you but of the whole world. Don’t disappoint me.”

— Pakistan Studies 9 (PESRP), Class 9, 2012, p. 28

The two leaders met at Jinnah’s residence on Malabar Hill in Bombay from 9 to 27 September 1944.

The textbooks narrate the failure of the talks through Jinnah’s harsh assessment: asserting that Quaid-e-Azam adjudged Gandhi’s style as “nothing but cheating and hypocrisy and cunningness.”

“In this very meeting Gandhi said clearly that he represented nobody but himself... during the talks, Gandhi refused to accept the Two-Nation Theory... Quaid-e-Azam adjudged that the style adopted by Gandhi is nothing but cheating and hypocrisy and cunningness.”

— Pakistan Studies 9 (PESRP), Class 9, 2012, p. 29

  1. Having resigned from the Congress in October 1934, Gandhi explicitly wrote to Jinnah: “I have approached you as an individual, seeking to find common ground.” Jinnah legitimately countered that an agreement signed with an unmandated individual could not legally bind the Congress Working Committee.
  2. While Jinnah rejected Gandhi’s proposed terms (which offered a plebiscite on separation only after the British departed an undivided central administration), the words “cheating, hypocrisy, and cunningness” (fareb, munāfiqat aur chālbāzī) never appear in Jinnah’s published letters, verbatim transcripts, or official press conferences.
  3. Jinnah’s actual public statement on 4 October 1944 was that Gandhi offered a “shadow and a husk, a maimed, mutilated and moth-eaten Pakistan.”
IX

Partition, Princely States, and the Kashmir

On 3 June 1947, Lord Mountbatten announced the formal partition plan, establishing Pakistan and India as independent dominions on 14–15 August 1947. What followed were the bitter post-Partition conflicts surrounding the princely states—Junagadh and Kashmir, as well as financial assets disputes.

On the contested accession of Junagadh, textbooks report: “India promptly invited a relative of Mr. Gandhi to form an exiled government of Junagadh.”

“India promptly invited a relative of Mr. Gandhi to form an exiled government of Junagadh.”

— Tārīkh-e-Pākistān, Class 9–10, 1986, p. 268

  1. The “relative” was Samaldas Gandhi, Mohandas Gandhi’s nephew (the son of his elder brother Laxmidas Gandhi). Samaldas was a Gujarati journalist and politician based in Bombay.
  2. When Nawab Mahabat Khan III acceded Junagadh to Pakistan on 15 August 1947, Samaldas Gandhi formed the Arzi Hukumat (Provisional Government) of Junagadh in Bombay on 25 September 1947 to organize resistance.
  3. Gandhi had no operational command or strategic control over the Arzi Hukumat. The paramilitary and diplomatic campaign was directed by Vallabhbhai Patel’s Ministry of States and V. P. Menon.

On Kashmir, the 1986 History textbook links Gandhi’s August 1947 visit directly to the Maharaja’s subsequent crackdown on the Muslim population.

یکم اگست 1947ء کو مسٹر گاندھی نے کشمیر کا دورہ کیا اور مہاراجہ کشمیر سے ملاقات کی ۔ اس واقعہ کے چند دنوں بعد مہاراجہ کشمیر نے اپنی مسلم رعایا کو جلد از جلد نہتا کر دینے کی کوشش تیز کر دی ۔

(“On 1 August 1947, Mr. Gandhi visited Kashmir and met the Maharaja of Kashmir. A few days after this event, the Maharaja of Kashmir accelerated efforts to disarm his Muslim subjects as quickly as possible.”)

— Tārīkh-e-Pākistān, Class 9–10, 1986, p. 269

  1. Gandhi visited Kashmir at the request of the Congress Working Committee and Viceroy Mountbatten to assess the political crisis. In meetings with Maharaja Hari Singh and Prime Minister Ram Chandra Kak, Gandhi explicitly stated that neither the British Crown nor the Maharaja possessed the moral right to decide Kashmir’s fate: the will of the people of Jammu and Kashmir must reign supreme.
  2. Gandhi publicly declared that if the Kashmiri people decided to join Pakistan, no power could legitimately prevent them.
  3. Gandhi’s primary intervention was to urge for the immediate release of political prisoners, notably Sheikh Abdullah of the National Conference, who had been imprisoned for demanding democratic constitutional rights.

The 2019 Class 12 textbook contains the only moment in the entire corpus where Gandhi is credited with an act that benefited Pakistan, albeit framed with cynical motives: claiming that “for fear of being shamed before the international community,” Gandhi asked India to release Pakistan’s assets.

پاکستان کے 750 ملین روپے کے اثاثہ جات روک لیے گئے... اس موقع پر گاندھی نے بین الاقوامی برادری کے سامنے شرمندہ ہونے کے خوف سے کہا کہ پاکستان کو یہ رقم ادا کر دی جائے۔ چنانچہ بھارت نے تذبذب کے ساتھ 500 ملین روپے کی ایک قسط ادا کر دی۔

(“Pakistan's assets of 750 million rupees were withheld... At this juncture, Gandhi, for fear of being shamed before the international community, said that this money should be paid to Pakistan. Consequently, India hesitantly paid an instalment of 500 million rupees.”)

— Mutāla'ah Pākistān 12, Class 12, 2019, p. 32

  1. Pakistan’s agreed share of undivided reserve cash balances was Rs 750 million (75 crore). Rs 200 million (20 crore) had already been paid upon Partition in August 1947. The withheld balance was Rs 550 million (55 crore), not 500 million.
  2. Deputy Prime Minister Vallabhbhai Patel openly declared on 12 January 1948 that India would withhold the Rs 55 crore until the Kashmir conflict was resolved, using financial starvation as military leverage.
  3. There is no evidence that Gandhi acted from “fear of international shame.” On 12 January 1948, Gandhi commenced an indefinite fast unto death in Delhi, demanding two moral concessions: the restoration of peace and mosques for Delhi’s besieged Muslims, and the immediate, unconditional release of the Rs 55 crore owed to Pakistan as a sacred national debt. Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Patel reversed their decision on 15 January 1948 and transferred the full Rs 55 crore to Pakistan’s central account.
  4. The fatal consequence: On 30 January 1948, Nathuram Godse shot Gandhi dead at Birla House. Godse explicitly cited Gandhi’s fast forcing the transfer of the 55 crore to Pakistan as his primary justification for the murder.

The Four Erasures & Summative Appraisals

In 1979 and 1980 we do see a rare positive comparison. In a biographical profile of Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar, both textbooks declare: “He belongs to the generation of Mr. Gandhi and Quaid-i-Azam. He is almost as great as they were.”

With the tragic events of January 1948, Gandhi vanishes from the chronological timeline of Pakistani schoolbooks.

Yet his pedagogical utility within the educational architecture of the state was far from exhausted. Having mobilized Gandhi as an active adversary across five decades of political struggle, the curriculum periodically steps back to render summative appraisals, positioning Gandhi as the ultimate ideological foil against which Jinnah’s historical greatness is measured.

In a 1987 Intermediate Pakistan Studies text, Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah is quoted addressing the Muslim League Council in February 1940:

نہ ہم انگریز کی چودھراہٹ قبول کریں گے اور نہ ہی مسٹر گاندھی اور کانگرس کی بالادستی۔ ہم دونوں کے اثر سے آزاد رہنا چاہتے ہیں۔

(“We shall let neither Britain establish its authority over us, nor Mr. Gandhi and the Congress. We wish to remain free of the influence of both.”)

— Muṭāla‘ah Pākistān, Intermediate (Class 11), 1987, p. 13

When evaluating how Pakistani textbooks construct Gandhi in summative terms, what is excluded is as revealing as what is included. Across our entire textbook corpus, four foundational dimensions of Gandhi’s life and historical reality are subjected to complete erasure:

  1. He has no birth year or geographic origin: We are never told that he was born in 1869 in Porbandar, Gujarat—the very same province where Jinnah’s family had their origins. Across all 808 sentences, his birth year never once appears.
  2. He has no youth or foreign formation: His legal education at the Inner Temple in London and his formative twenty-one years of civil rights activism in South Africa (1893–1914), where his political consciousness, legal strategy, and non-violent resistance were first forged, are completely erased.
  3. His philosophy is hollowed out into a trick: The core philosophical doctrines of ahimsa (non-violence) and satyāgraha (soul-force / truth-force) are never engaged as moral systems in their own right. Instead, ahimsa is dismissed across textbooks as a cynical political tactic or a convenient “pretext” (bahānā) utilized to suspend mass movements whenever Muslim interests were ascendant.
  4. He is never assassinated: The violent termination of his life on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist extremist affiliated with the Hindu Mahasabha and RSS, is never acknowledged in the historical narrative—because acknowledging that Gandhi died protecting Delhi Muslims and forcing the payment of 55 crore to Pakistan would irreparably collapse his designated role as the state’s irredeemable communal villain.

Instead of understanding Gandhi as a complex historical actor, he enters the textbooks fully formed as an adult Congress politician, functioning exclusively as an obstacle, an adversary, and a foil to the Pakistan Movement.

The Pedagogical Foil in the Postcolonial State

One might assume that the dawn of the twenty-first century—accompanied by globalization, ubiquitous internet access, the rise of international educational standards, and multiple rounds of statutory curriculum reform—would have tempered this rigid oppositional paradigm. Yet an empirical examination of Pakistan’s most recent reform initiative, the Single National Curriculum (SNC), reveals that the construction of Gandhi as the state’s structural foil remains remarkably resilient.

گاندھی کی دوغلی پالیسیوں کی وجہ سے مسلمان معاشی طور پر بری طرح تباہ ہوئے۔

(“Due to Gandhi’s duplicitous policies, Muslims were economically ruined.”)

— Civics 12 (SNC), Class 12, 2023, p. 24

The persistence and thematic consistency of Gandhi’s depiction has endured across disparate political orders: the modernist military dictatorship of Ayub Khan (1958–1969), the Islamic socialist democracy of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto (1971–1977), the aggressive Islamization under General Zia-ul-Haq’s martial regime (1977–1988), the alternating civilian parliamentary governments of the 1990s, General Pervez Musharraf’s era of “Enlightened Moderation” (1999–2008), and the Single National Curriculum under Imran Khan’s government (2018–2022).

Gandhi and Jinnah do not exist as independent historical actors, but as the structural, interdependent halves of a single ideological equation.

The textbooks present students with a sanctified Jinnah operating in a vacuum of moral purity, equipping students with an immutable verdict rather than historical comprehension: a historical figure who can only be venerated cannot be defended. The real, empirical Jinnah was a far more formidable political figure than the cardboard saint of state textbooks: a master constitutional lawyer, a senior member of the Indian National Congress, the brilliant architect of the 1916 Lucknow Pact, and a statesman once celebrated by Gopal Krishna Gokhale and Sarojini Naidu as the “ambassador of Hindu-Muslim unity.”

Jinnah did not arrive at the demand for Pakistan through mystical revelation or primordial religious antipathy, but through twenty-five years of exhausting, unyielding, and ultimately frustrated constitutional negotiations to secure statutory minority safeguards within a united Indian federation. That historical reality explains why Pakistan became an existential necessity; the hagiographic version merely asserts it as dogma.

In contrast, Gandhi is reduced to an unnuanced archetype of communal deceit, bad faith, and Hindu Mahasabha puppetry.

This dual portrait reveals a profound epistemic law of postcolonial nation-building: the hagiography of the national hero is strictly coupled to the cartoonish flattening of his constitutive antagonist. If Gandhi possessed no legitimate constituency, no authentic anti-imperial strategy, and no genuine political dilemmas, then Jinnah’s monumental historical achievement shrinks into merely out-manoeuvring a two-dimensional cartoon.

The textbook mechanism ultimately traps national identity in a logic of identity by subtraction. As captured in the 1987 Class 11 civics text (p. 13), Jinnah is framed in February 1940 as declaring that Muslims must be freed simultaneously from British imperialism and Gandhi’s designs. When a nation’s founding selfhood is defined strictly in the negative—by who it is against rather than what it aspires to construct—the postcolonial state remains hostage to the very foil it seeks to repudiate.

In an interconnected digital world, Pakistani students inevitably encounter Mohandas Gandhi outside the textbook board monopoly, discovering his global stature in anticolonial thought, his profound influence on Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela, and his martyrdom at the hands of Hindu extremists for defending Muslim financial and physical rights.

When the textbook’s caricature fails to survive contact with reality, the resulting skepticism does not stay confined to Gandhi. It inadvertently undermines student trust in the legitimate historical grievances documented in the same books: the documented atrocities of 1947, the injustice of the Kashmir accession, and the systemic socioeconomic marginalization of Indian Muslims under colonial and postcolonial rule.
